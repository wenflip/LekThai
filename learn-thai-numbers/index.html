How to Learn Thai Numbers

Thai numbers are a small, closed, regular system — about eleven words and two exceptions. That means the problem is not understanding them. It is recall speed: getting from "I know this" to "I heard that and I knew it instantly." Those need different practice, and most people only ever do the first.

Why numbers are worth learning first

If you are learning Thai for real life rather than for a certificate, numbers give you more usable ability per hour than almost anything else:

  • They come up constantly — prices, times, addresses, phone numbers, room numbers, bus routes.
  • They are finite. Vocabulary is endless; numbers are a closed set you can genuinely finish.
  • They are regular, so learning eleven words unlocks every number to a million.
  • They are the most common point of real-world failure. Freezing when a driver names a fare is a specific, fixable problem.

The system, in one screen

Learn 1 to 10, learn that สิบ is ten, and assemble: tens word, then units word. สามสิบสี่ = 34.

Then the two exceptions, which are the entire irregularity of the language's number system:

  • Trailing 1 above ten → เอ็ด. 11 is สิบเอ็ด.
  • 2 in the tens place → ยี่. 20 is ยี่สิบ.

Above that, place-value words: ร้อย 100, พัน 1,000, หมื่น 10,000, แสน 100,000. Full tables are on the Thai numbers guide and the 1–100 list.

That is the knowledge. You can read it in five minutes. It will not make you fluent in numbers, and that gap is what the rest of this page is about.

Why reading the table does not work

Reviewing a list feels productive because everything looks familiar. Familiarity is not recall. The moment the answer is not on screen, the knowledge often is not there either.

Two findings from memory research do most of the work here:

  • Retrieval practice. Being tested on something strengthens memory far more than re-reading it. The effort of dragging the answer out is the mechanism, so easy review is close to wasted time.
  • Spaced repetition. Reviews spread over days beat the same total time crammed into one session. Being on the edge of forgetting is what makes the next recall stick.

This is the same principle behind flashcard tools like Anki. The difference with numbers is that the set is tiny and completely regular, so you do not need a heavyweight system — a few focused minutes a day genuinely finishes the job.

Recognition and production are two different skills

This is where most self-study goes wrong. There are two directions, and being good at one does not make you good at the other:

  • Production — you see 47 and produce สี่สิบเจ็ด. This is what tables train, and it is the easier direction.
  • Recognition — someone says สี่สิบเจ็ด at conversational speed and you know it is 47. This is the one that fails in real life, because there is no text, no pause, and no second attempt.

You need both, and you should deliberately spend more time on recognition than feels necessary. If you only ever practise reading numbers, you will meet your first real spoken price and discover you have trained the wrong skill.

A seven-day plan

Ten minutes a day. Short and daily beats long and occasional — the spacing is doing real work.

DayFocusWhat to do
11–10Learn the ten words cold. Say each aloud with audio. Do not move on until you can produce all ten without looking.
211–20Add สิบ and the เอ็ด rule. Mix in yesterday's numbers rather than practising the teens alone.
3The tens20 through 90, and the ยี่ exception. Now you can build anything under 100.
41–100, listeningSwitch direction. Listen and type. Expect this to feel much harder than day 3 did — that is the point.
5HundredsAdd ร้อย and drill realistic prices: 120, 250, 500, 800.
6ThousandsAdd พัน, then หมื่น and แสน. Slow down here — the regrouping is the real hurdle.
7Mixed, timedFull range, listening only, at speed. Then keep going a few minutes a day until it is boring.

The five mistakes

  1. Learning to read but not to hear. The most common and the most costly. Spoken numbers are what you will actually meet.
  2. Studying in long sessions. An hour on Sunday is worth less than ten minutes daily. Spacing is not a scheduling detail; it is the mechanism.
  3. Practising in order. Counting 1, 2, 3 up to 100 trains a sequence, not the numbers. You end up unable to produce 57 without running up to it. Always randomise.
  4. Skipping the awkward ones. Everybody has three or four that stay slippery. Those are exactly the ones needing more reps, and the ones people quietly avoid.
  5. Stopping at 100. Prices, salaries and rents live in the thousands, and หมื่น/แสน are where English speakers stall hardest.

Practising outside the app

Numbers have an unusual advantage: the real world is full of free practice material.

  • Read prices in Thai in your head while shopping — anywhere, not just in Thailand.
  • Say the time in Thai whenever you check your phone.
  • Read your bus, flight or receipt numbers in Thai.
  • In Thailand: listen for the price before looking at the till display, then check yourself. That is a proper retrieval test with instant feedback.

What "done" looks like

Not "I can work it out." The target is: someone says a number and you know it, without translating, without a pause. For 1–100 most people get there within a couple of weeks of short daily sessions. Thousands take a little longer, mostly because of the regrouping.

After that it is maintenance — a couple of minutes occasionally, and it stays.

Questions

How long does it take to learn Thai numbers?

With ten minutes a day, most learners can produce 1 to 100 reliably within about a week, and recognise them spoken at natural speed within two to three weeks. Thousands take a little longer because of the regrouping into หมื่น and แสน.

What is the fastest way to learn Thai numbers?

Short daily sessions of randomised, audio-first testing, weighted towards the numbers you keep getting wrong. Retrieval practice and spaced repetition beat re-reading a table by a wide margin.

Should I learn Thai numbers before anything else?

They are one of the highest-value starting points for practical Thai, because the set is finite, regular, and comes up constantly in real situations. They pair well with basic greetings and politeness particles.

Do I need to learn the Thai numerals too?

Not urgently. Thailand mostly uses Arabic numerals day to day. The ten Thai symbols take about ten minutes and are useful on official documents, temple signage and dual-price boards.

Why can I read Thai numbers but not understand them when spoken?

Because reading and listening are different skills. Reading gives you time and text; listening gives you neither. The fix is deliberate listen-and-answer practice rather than more table review.

Is a flashcard app like Anki good for Thai numbers?

The underlying principles are right, but numbers are a small regular system rather than thousands of separate facts, so a dedicated drill with built-in audio and randomisation is usually faster to set up and more suited to the task.
